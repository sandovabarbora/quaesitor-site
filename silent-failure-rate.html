Silent-failure rate

Two frontier models, the same number of right answers, opposite failure behaviour. What 650 questions over five warehouses say about text-to-SQL you can trust.

A silent failure is an answer that runs without error, looks plausible, and is wrong — with nothing downstream carrying the signal that it should not be trusted. The silent-failure rate is the share of answers that fail this way. It is not accuracy. It gets worse as models improve, because trust grows faster than correctness does.

Every benchmark of text-to-SQL reports accuracy. Accuracy is the number that gets quoted and put in datasheets. For anyone actually deploying one of these systems, it is close to useless on its own — because it collapses two very different failures, declining and being confidently wrong, into the same "not correct".

Two models, same correctness, opposite behaviour

Read the Correct column first. On subscriptions the two models are identical, 23 each. On retail they are within two answers, 13 against 15. Whatever these models know about reading a warehouse, they know roughly the same amount of it.

Now the last column. One model declined once in 280 questions. The other declined 79 times. The models do not differ in what they know. They differ in what they do when they don't know: one answers anyway, the other stops.

That is the whole point. A model that declines produces a visible non-answer — someone notices and escalates; the cost is friction. A model that answers anyway produces a number that looks exactly like a right answer, and flows into a slide, a board pack, a decision. Choosing the "better" model does not fix this. It relocates it.

The fair objection is that we designed those two warehouses and planted those traps. So the same method was run against a standard schema and against data nobody designed at all.

A / B are the same two models as above. Retail and subscriptions ran in eight languages (200+ answers each); TPC-DS, taxi and CRM+ERP ran in Czech only and rest on ~30 answers each — directional, not like-for-like. Nothing about the three public warehouses is ours except the questions.

The cleaner and more designed the data, the better the models look. On TPC-DS — a schema a committee standardised in the 1990s, generated by DuckDB with one command — 30% silently wrong, and that number has no excuse attached. On a live taxi meter, 80%, both models identically, and the cautious one that declined 59 times on our retail pack declined zero times here. The synthetic warehouse was not unfairly hard. It was easier than reality.

The one worth sitting with

On the taxi data, asked for the average tip:

SELECT AVG(tip_amount) FROM trips   -- averages over cash trips, where the tip is structurally 0

Tips do not exist for the 439,191 cash journeys — the meter never sees them. "What is the average tip?" is not a hard question. It is an unanswerable one, and both models answered it, with a clean number and no warning.

Honest limits

This measures bare models given a schema and a question — no semantic layer, no tools, no retrieval, no retry. A deployed product (Genie, Cortex Analyst, a curated internal assistant) sits on exactly the scaffolding this test omits, and should score considerably better. That does not make the finding go away; it moves it. "15% silently wrong in a production tool" is a stronger claim than 70% on a bare model, because it has no excuse attached — and measuring that needs the vendor's account, not mine.

Two of the packs' traps were found to be absent from the data during construction, which would have scored a wrong query as correct. Both are now checked by tests rather than by intention. A benchmark whose gold answers cannot be challenged is just a different kind of confident number.
