What gets inspected

Four layers, in this order.

The entry audit covers the first two and outlines the third. Each layer answers a different question, and they are ordered by how expensive the failure is to discover late.

  • I. Is the answer right? Verified SQL written independently for every question, then the same question in five wordings. Five different numbers means the system is unreliable even if one of them is correct.
  • II. Was that person allowed to see it? Row security bypassed through a join path, an individual recovered from an aggregate, a masked column derived from a computed one, identity taken from the prompt instead of from authentication.
  • III. Can it be talked around? Instructions planted in the question, and instructions planted in the data itself: query results come back into the model's context, so a row in a table can carry an instruction.
  • IV. Does it still hold next quarter? The suite from the first three layers is frozen and run on every change to a prompt, a model, a schema or a definition. Without this an audit is a photograph, not a control.

Layer one is where the money is. Layer four is why it stays found.

An audit that is not repeated is a rumour with a date on it.

Written for your trade

The traps are not the same in every business.

A retailer loses money to a join that multiplies rows. A subscription business loses it to a yearly invoice booked in the month it was raised. Both answers look ordinary on a slide, and neither is caught by a test that only asks whether the query ran.

Each trade gets its own set of questions, and behind every question is a verified answer written by hand. Five sets exist today. If yours is not among them, the audit is where it gets written, and it stays with you afterwards, in your repository, as the suite that keeps the answer true.

When teams call us

Usually at one of these moments.

  • 01Someone asked where a number came from, and putting the answer together took three days.
  • 02You are about to open the assistant to everyone, not just the people who can read SQL.
  • 03An auditor, a regulator or a customer asked how you know the figures are right, and you would like a better answer than trust.
  • 04You want to be sure a wrong number would be caught before it reaches a report, a forecast or an invoice.

None of these means anything is broken. They are all the same gap: the answer arrives without anything attached that would let you check it. That gap is new, and it is closeable.

Before you ask

What people ask before they buy.

Several of these are reasons not to buy. They are answered here rather than on the call, because you should be able to rule us out without spending half an hour on it.

We already have dbt tests.

Those test the data: uniqueness, not-null, referential integrity, freshness. They pass on every finding in the sample report, because none of them is a broken row. The failure is that a correct query answered a different question than the one that was asked.

Our vendor has benchmarked it.

In English, on a warehouse that is not yours, by the party being measured. That is not a criticism of them; it is the reason penetration testing is bought from a third party rather than from the people who wrote the code.

Our data team knows the schema better than you will.

They do, and that is the difficulty. They wrote the definitions under test, so a review by them checks the implementation against their own intent. No team independently verifies its own semantics; that is what independence means, not that we know your business better.

What makes a query verified?

Not that we knew the right answer in advance. On your warehouse nobody does; if somebody did, there would be nothing to audit. Verified means the number has a provenance you can follow, and it is built from three things in this order.

The definition comes from you, in writing. We do not decide what revenue means in your company. Whoever owns the metric states it, and the question does not enter the suite until they have. A surprising amount of the value is here: it is often written down nowhere, and two departments answer differently. That disagreement is a finding before a line of SQL exists.

The query implements that definition, and the mapping between the two is read through with the person who owns it, clause by clause. What is being checked at this stage is narrow and worth stating: that the query says what the definition says.

The result is anchored. It is reconciled against a number you already trust and that was produced independently of us: a closed month, a filed statement, an ERP total, a report somebody signs. Where no such anchor exists, the query is written twice, independently, and the two are compared. If they disagree, that is a finding, and it is settled before the question is used to judge anything.

The part that is fair to hold against us: if a verified query is wrong, every finding built on it is wrong, and it looks exactly like one that is right. That is our own silent failure, and there is no clever defence against it, only the suite being yours, in your repository, with every query readable and re-runnable. Being told that a verified query is wrong is the most useful thing anyone can do for this method.

Could we not just run this ourselves?

Yes, and the suite is handed over precisely so you can, every quarter, without us. What takes the three weeks is not running it. It is writing a verified query for each of the questions your business actually asks, and being sure that query is right.

The part to be clear-eyed about is what happens afterwards. The suite keeps testing the traps it was written for, and it will keep catching them for as long as you run it. What it cannot do is notice a trap nobody has written yet, and those arrive on their own: the model version moves under you, a column is renamed, someone redefines a metric, a new source system lands. A suite that is never extended does not fail loudly. It goes on passing, against a warehouse it no longer describes.

That is what continuous verification is for, and it is also the honest reason not to buy it: if your schema and your model are genuinely static, running the suite yourself each quarter is enough.

What is the difference between the four packages?

Entry audit answers whether you have a problem and how big it is: the first two layers, the third in outline, and a written verdict. Three weeks, fixed price. Most people start and stop here, and that is a legitimate outcome.

Full audit is the same work carried through to a fix: all four layers, the suite handed over in a repository you own, help wiring it into your CI, and one retest after you have remediated. It is the entry audit plus the part where the findings actually get closed. The handed-over suite records the state as of the handover date. It is yours to run, but it does not cover traps, model versions or schema changes that arrive after.

Continuous verification is what keeps that from ageing. Models change under you, schemas drift, someone redefines a metric; the suite is re-run on each change you tell us about, and its coverage is extended each quarter. The signature holds only for a suite that is maintained; without it the verdict expires and the audit is a photograph with a date on it.

Annual verification is the yearly review against the controls, ending in a written statement carrying a signature: the document you hand to a board, an auditor or a customer who asked. It is our finding, not an accredited certificate, and it says so on its face. It presumes the suite from the full audit already exists and runs.

They stack in that order, and nobody should buy the fourth without the second. Only the entry audit carries a number here. What the rest cost depends on how many questions your business actually asks, how many warehouses or source systems have to agree, and whether a semantic layer already stands between the model and the tables. The honest answer is that the entry audit is what establishes those. Quoting them before it has run would be quoting work nobody has scoped yet.

Why does it cost that much?

Because what is being bought is not weeks of somebody's time at a rate. It is a verified answer to each question your business actually asks, which means deciding what revenue means here, in your company, where four columns could all be called that and they disagree; writing the query that implements the decision; and proving it is right before anything is trusted. That is the work, and it does not get faster with a better model.

Set against what it prevents, the arithmetic is not close. One misstated figure in a board pack, a forecast or an invoice run costs more than the audit, and the ones that matter are the ones nothing flags. But that is exactly the test to apply: if your assistant answers a handful of questions for a couple of people, and a wrong answer would be noticed and shrugged off, this is too expensive and you should not buy it. The price assumes the answers reach decisions.

What do you need from us?

Read access to a copy of the warehouse, or to the warehouse itself under a restricted role. A few hours from somebody who knows what the columns mean, spread over the three weeks. Nothing else, and nothing leaves your side.

Where does our data go during an audit?

Nowhere. The suite runs against your warehouse, in your infrastructure, under your credentials, and three things follow from that.

Your assistant is driven through your own deployment. We do not put your data into a model account of ours, so we add no processor you have not already contracted with. The system under test answers on your keys, exactly as it does for your staff, which is also the only way to measure the system you actually run rather than a copy of it.

Where our tooling needs a model of its own, it runs locally. The harness speaks to a model on the machine doing the work, not to a hosted API. Nothing is sent anywhere to be paraphrased or classified.

The verified queries are written by hand. The reference a finding is measured against is human work, not a second model's opinion, which is why it can be signed.

What if you find nothing serious?

Then the report says so, with the runs attached, and you have a document that answers the board's question. That is a result, and it is the one we would rather deliver. We say the same on the call if it looks likely beforehand.
